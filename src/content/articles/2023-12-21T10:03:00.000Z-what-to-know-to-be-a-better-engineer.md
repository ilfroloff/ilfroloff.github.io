➡️
[https://www.spicyweb.dev/the-great-gaslighting-of-the-js-age/](https://www.spicyweb.dev/the-great-gaslighting-of-the-js-age/)

I won't retell it, because it's better to feel the author's pain yourself, but
the message is this - often young (and not only) developers voluntarily become
hostages of one or a small number of technologies (the article uses React as an
example) and consider them the ideal/future/\[any admirable adjective\]. They
have little desire to learn anything different from their ideal and even
consider it harmful.

The problem is deep and relates not only to React and not only to the web, but
to absolutely any technology a developer loves. Subjectivity is a very poor (and
even somewhat destructive) quality for a developer. Yes, there may be
technologies closer to the heart, but they shouldn't influence objectivity when
making decisions. The evaluation and selection of final technologies should come
from requirements, timelines, and team level (in order of importance).

Personally, I'm of the opinion that a programmer should first and foremost
understand general principles and approaches - this is needed for
professionalism, while specific technologies like React are "for the soul".
General principles and approaches don't depend on where they're applied - web,
desktop, mobile, IoT, low-level, or even hardware development. The value of such
a programmer is enormous - an incredible speed of adaptation to changes, and
software development is one of the most dynamic fields.

But what do these "general principles and approaches" mean?

## General principles and approaches

These are all the knowledge and principles accumulated by the best minds over
decades of software development and confirmed many times in practice. This is a
huge topic and it's impossible to cover it in a short post, but I can try to
provide a path to begin the immersion. Below I've outlined the main directions
for development in order of complexity of absorption and breadth of material.
I've also added the most useful links to relevant literature to start diving in.

#### 1. SOLID DRY KISS for YAGNI

If someone saw meaninglessness in this sentence, doesn't know who the girl Yagni
is and why anyone would kiss her, don't worry. Each word is an abbreviation of a
development principle. Study them individually and it will be a huge boost to
you as a professional. The main generalized message of the phrase is
structuring, unambiguity, and clarity of written code.

A short list of literature where you can study these principles in more detail:

**SOLID** - first and foremost, go to Robert Martin's books, as he introduced
this term into common usage, specifically:

[Agile Principles, Patterns, and Practices in C#](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0131857258) -
an excellent book. Don't be put off by the C# focus - the examples and
explanations provided are universal for any language. SOLID gets its own section
in the book, where each letter is thoroughly explained. The book is worthy of
being read from cover to cover - the concepts and ideas described in it are
fundamental and don't depend on language or platform.

**DRY** - similar situation, first and foremost go to the book that introduced
the term:

[The Pragmatic Programmer: From Journeyman to Master](https://www.amazon.com/dp/020161622X/) -
timeless classic. The book is practical, the book is philosophical. A look at
programming as an art, where you're turned into a wonderful and subtle artist.
What a professional needs.

**KISS / YAGNI** - here it's best to turn to books on code cleanliness.

[Refactoring: Improving the Design of Existing Code](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0134757599/)<br/>
[Clean Code: A Handbook of Agile Software Craftsmanship](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0132350882/) -
both books are recipe references for improving your code. Not all "recipes" need
to be agreed with, but the books' messages are correct and needed, which is why
they're rightly considered classics. If you see "analog" books somewhere,
they'll most likely say the same thing just in different words, so it's better
to start with these two.

And a couple of books that can't be attributed to a specific principle, but
conceptually it's about the same:

[Code Complete: A Practical Handbook of Software Construction](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0735619670/) -
a must-have for beginner (and not only) programmers. It instills good and proper
habits from the first steps. Not everything in this book may be to your liking,
you may not entirely want to agree, but that's how it should be. It teaches
independence and the development of your own style and sense of beauty.

[A Philosophy of Software Design](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1732102201/) - a
book not for beginners, but for those seeking new perspectives on development
and system building. The book is a mix of various topics, so you can read only
the sections that interest you. The author's view on some questions doesn't
align with Fowler's, for example, but that's great - the more different
opinions, the broader your horizons. Our goal is to make you a professional, and
a professional is never fixated on a single opinion.

#### 2. Data Structures and Algorithms

Knowledge of data structures and algorithms helps you write efficient, concise
code that other programmers can understand. As you study structures and
algorithms, you'll notice how understanding and comprehension of the
technologies and tools you use every day will come. Information will start
falling into the right places in your mind. By the way, when applying for jobs
at FAANG-level companies - this is a must-have, and these folks won't ask for
useless knowledge.

For structures and partially algorithms, there's a free and excellent course on
the Stepik platform ([link](https://stepik.org/course/579/)). But in general,
this is exactly the case where the best way to learn is through practice. For
this, [LeetCode](https://leetcode.com/) or its analogs work perfectly. Usually,
on these services there's a list of various practical problems on algorithmics
and data structures with the ability to evaluate your solution and with an
explanation of the best approach for the problem being solved.

It's hard to suggest anything specific from books, as there are countless many,
but for me the most useful were:

[Algorithms Illuminated](https://www.algorithmsilluminated.org/) - a cycle of
books and videos on algorithms (books at the bottom of the site). The
mathematical foundation of the main algorithms is explained quite well. The
books are structured as a reference, so you can choose the algorithm you need
and study it specifically (only the first part has an introductory overview of
general algorithmic mathematics). Some of the algorithms are covered in the
Stepik course mentioned above.

[Introduction to Algorithms](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0262033844/) - I'll say
right away that the book is complex and not for bedtime reading, but rather for
sleeping But there's probably no book more detailed on algorithms, their
creation, and mathematical analysis. If algorithms are needed as part of general
knowledge, this book will probably be excessive.

#### 3. Design Patterns

Many have heard of them, but far from all use them. And that's a shame, because
these are ready-made solutions for common problems that have stood the test of
time and an infinite number of programmers. Patterns are abstract, so they're
applicable regardless of programming language or paradigm. Yes, there are many
patterns, and you can't remember them all, but you don't need to. Think of
patterns as a reference with answers to general questions, but one that you need
to at least skim through to be aware of what questions exist and what ready-made
answers are offered. In practice, you need to know about 7-10 of the commonly
used ones.

[Design Patterns: Elements of Reusable Object-Oriented Software](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0201633612/) -
eternal classic, the unmatched original. All the main patterns are formulated
and documented in this book. Perhaps too strict and formal, so below are a few
more modernized variants:

[Agile Principles, Patterns, and Practices in C#](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0131857258/) -
I already recommended this book above, but here there's a separate section with
the main and most "practical" patterns.

[Refactoring.Guru](https://refactoring.guru/design-patterns/) - a very
convenient site if you need to quickly recall the right pattern. With pictures,
examples, and references to other patterns. Just what you need for everyday use.

[Patterns.dev](https://www.patterns.dev/) - a reference of patterns written in
JavaScript and partially in React.

#### 4. Software Architecture and Design

Any code you write is a small part of something bigger. And seeing this bigger
picture is exactly what knowledge and understanding of various architectural
styles and designs will help with. So microservices, DDD, EDD, etc. are
precisely the terminology of this layer. You can study it endlessly, because
this layer essentially includes knowledge from the previous ones, as well as
knowledge to see how to actually assemble your final application from disparate
parts. There are many books, and even more information on the internet. What was
useful to me:

[Fundamentals of Software Architecture: An Engineering Approach](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1492043451/)<br/>
[Software Architecture: The Hard Parts](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1492086894/)<br/>
[Building Evolutionary Architectures: Automated Software Governance](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1492097543/) -
I discovered these books by chance, but their contents turned out to be
invaluable. The books explain what system architecture is and the role of the
architect in a project's life. They're written very vividly and interestingly,
with a large number of useful notes from the everyday work life of developers
and architects. The books will tell you how to turn your project into a
well-maintainable and working product. There's also a reference of ready-made
architectural solutions with details on how and where to apply them. In general,
excellent reading.

[Clean Architecture: A Craftsman's Guide to Software Structure and Design](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0134494164/) -
a good, lively book. Maybe not as fundamental as one would like, but you can
extract useful things from it. I love Martin's writing style, so I couldn't help
but add it

[Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0321127420/) -
a reference book of ready-made architectural solutions from Fowler. The
solutions are narrowly applicable, but that doesn't make them bad. They
partially overlap with [Fowler's blog](https://martinfowler.com/), which is also
worth following and reading.

[Implementing Domain-Driven Design](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0321834577/) - a
complete immersion in DDD. Today it's one of the main concepts in building
systems, and this book will help with that. DDD will come up during the study of
many architectural solutions. The book is grounded and practical, well revealing
what DDD is. The author will tell you why your vision of architecture should be
driven by business eyes and how to turn your architecture into one that solves
business problems.

In the context of DDD, it's worth mentioning the book
[Domain-Driven Design: Tackling Complexity in the Heart of Software](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0321125215/),
as it's the original source of the term, but the author's style can be somewhat
dry and strict.

[systemdesign.one](https://systemdesign.one/) - a wonderful blog on system
design. Here you'll find analysis of the design and architecture of the most
highly loaded applications in the world. Lots of examples and patterns. An
excellent resource for diving deeper into the topic.
